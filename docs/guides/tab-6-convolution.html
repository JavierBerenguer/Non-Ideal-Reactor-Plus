Tab 6 - Convolution

Use Convolution to predict the outlet tracer signal from an inlet signal and a residence-time distribution (RTD). Use Deconvolution to estimate the RTD from measured inlet and outlet signals.

Both modes put the signals on one common time grid and keep input units separate from display units.

Before You Start

Choose Convolution when...

  • You know Cin(t) and E(t).
  • You want the predicted Cout(t).
  • You want to check tracer area, mean-time, and variance relationships through one vessel.

Choose Deconvolution when...

  • You measured Cin(t) and Cout(t).
  • You need an estimated nonnegative E(t).
  • You can state a physically plausible delay range for the vessel response.
One vessel at a time: this tab estimates or applies one RTD. Vessel networks and series arrangements belong in the non-ideal tracer flowsheet; they are not a third mode here.

Convolution: Predict the Outlet Signal

Convolution applies the vessel RTD to the inlet history. The result is the outlet history that would be observed if the vessel were linear and time invariant under the test conditions.

  1. Select Convolution.
  2. Define Cin(t) and E(t) from a Table or Function; E can also use the RTD currently held in Tab 1 or an available ideal model.
  3. Choose Δtcommon and select Compute.
  4. Check the plotted alignment, the output table, and the area/mean/variance relationships before reusing the signal.

Deconvolution: Estimate the RTD

Deconvolution finds a nonnegative E(t) whose reconvolution with the inlet resembles the measured outlet. The estimate is constrained to the delay support from τmin to τmax. Use the narrowest range justified by the equipment and timing data: an unnecessarily wide support adds unknowns, while a range that is too narrow forces real response outside the model.

  1. Select Deconvolution and enter Cin(t) and measured Cout(t).
  2. Set τmin, τmax, and their unit from physical timing knowledge. The default is the widest support compatible with the signal windows.
  3. Start with Regularization: None for exact or teaching data. Select Compute.
  4. Use the lower Reconvolution check to compare measured points, the fitted outlet, and residual bars. Then inspect the relative residual and the E-area warning.

Tikhonov Regularization for Noisy Data

Deconvolution amplifies measurement noise. Select Tikhonov when an unregularized E follows point-to-point noise or contains implausibly sharp changes. Keep None for exact data unless there is a clear reason to smooth it: regularization can otherwise oversmooth the true RTD and increase the mismatch.

ChoiceUse it forInterpretation
Smoothing: second differenceThe usual noisy experimental RTD.Discourages curvature, producing a smoother shape while allowing its overall level to be determined by the data.
Smoothing: identityCases where limiting the magnitude is more appropriate than smoothing curvature.Penalizes the size of E itself and can shrink the whole estimate.
λ: Auto (L-curve)A defensible first choice when λ is unknown.Selects the marked corner that balances reconvolution error against the smoothing measure.
λ: ManualSensitivity studies or a value justified from repeated experiments.Enter the nonnegative strength directly; compare both the reconvolution and the resulting RTD as it changes.

Reconvolution check

Use it to see where the fitted outlet misses measured points. Structured residuals suggest a wrong support, baseline problem, timing mismatch, or model limitation rather than random noise alone.

L-curve

Use it to inspect the tradeoff behind automatic λ. The marked point is the selected balance, not proof that the RTD is physically correct; still check area, support, shape, and reconvolution.

How to Use Every Result

ResultMeaningEngineering use
AreaThe trapezoidal integral of each signal. For E, AE should be near 1; in convolution, Aout should equal AinAE.Checks tracer balance and whether the recorded window includes the full response. A mismatch can expose lost tracer, a baseline error, truncated tails, or inconsistent units.
τThe signal mean time; for an RTD it is the mean residence time. Mean times add in convolution: mean(Cout) = mean(Cin) + τE.Compare τE with V/Q. Qτ below nominal V indicates inactive or dead volume; an unexpectedly early response can indicate short-circuiting. Mean times also add across independent vessels in series.
σ2Variance in time units squared. Independent convolution variances add.With measurements immediately before and after a vessel, obtain its contribution by the two-point method: σE2 = σout2 - σin2. It separates spreading caused by the vessel from spreading already present at the inlet.
σθ2Dimensionless variance, σ2/τ2.Compares RTD breadth independently of vessel time scale. Use N ≈ 1/σθ2 as a tanks-in-series estimate. For axial dispersion, interpret breadth with the Peclet number Pe: high Pe means weak dispersion and a narrow RTD; low Pe means stronger dispersion and a broader RTD.
Relative residualThe reconvolution error relative to the measured outlet magnitude over the measured window.Read the displayed rating as a fit screen: below 5% is good, 5–15% is acceptable, and above 15% asks you to check data or E support. A small value does not by itself validate the RTD physically.
Warning AE ≠ 1The estimated E does not have unit area within the chosen support.Check tracer recovery, baselines, tails, units, and support before accepting it. Sending it to Tab 1 normalizes it, but normalization does not repair poor data or a wrong support.
λThe Tikhonov regularization strength; the result shows whether automatic selection used the L-curve.Larger λ favors the selected smoothing rule more strongly and usually sacrifices reconvolution fit. Compare nearby values manually when the automatic corner gives an implausible RTD.
